Trash to Treasure

A gamified upcycling app that teaches children sustainability by making — turning everyday trash into keepsakes worth keeping.

Role
UX Designer · team of 4
Timeline
2025 · HM Munich
Tools
Figma · FigJam
Scope
Research · IA · UI
Key outcome

A full UX process — 4 personas, an end-to-end journey map, user flow and IA — resolved into a gamified hi-fi prototype for children and parents.

01 — Problem

Sustainability is taught as a rule, not as something you make.

Children learn environmental values mostly through instruction, while their hands — and attention — go to screens. Parents want learning that feels real and non-digital; kids want play. Recycling, framed as a chore, rarely becomes a habit that sticks.

We designed for children roughly 6–12 and the parents alongside them.

How might we

…teach children sustainability through making — so that everyday trash becomes something worth keeping?

02 — Research

We started with real lives, not the product.

We interviewed children and parents, built empathy maps to capture each group's mindset, and distilled them into four personas. Crucially, we mapped the full user journey without our product in it — so the design would answer real needs, not assume itself.

03 — Define

Mapping the day before the app.

  1. Learn by making

    Every lesson is a thing the child builds with their hands.

  2. Reward progress, not perfection

    Badges and levels celebrate the trying, not the result.

  3. Bridge screen and real world

    The phone guides; the making happens off it.

04 — Ideate → Design

Three ways to play, one habit to build.

The app resolves into three modes: a Story Mode that teaches through adventure and badges, a Gallery Mode to pick a project to build, and a voice-assisted Step-by-Step Guide for kids who can't yet read fluently.

05 — Outcome

A lesson that feels like play.

We walked the prototype through with a small group of children to check whether the modes were learnable without an adult reading every screen.

What I learned

  • Designing for kids means designing for the parent standing behind them.
  • Keeping the product out of early research kept the team honest.
  • Voice and image carry more than text when your user can't read yet.

If I continued

  • Test the Step-by-Step Guide with a wider age range.
  • Add a parent dashboard for shared progress.
  • Prototype the physical collecting kit alongside the app.

My contribution

In a team of four, I led the research synthesis and information architecture — empathy maps, journey map, and user flow — and designed the Story Mode and Step-by-Step Guide screens in the hi-fi prototype.
